Market jargon, decoded.

50 trading and investing terms explained in plain English, from ISAs and gilts to pips, leverage and CPI. Written for UK investors, and kept short enough to read on the bus.

The basicsInvestingTradingEconomics and central banksCrypto

The basics

Asset

Anything you can buy that has a market value: shares, bonds, currencies, gold, property or crypto.

Bull market and bear market

A bull market is a long stretch of rising prices; a bear market is a sustained fall, usually defined as a drop of 20% or more from a recent high.

Ticker

The short code an asset trades under. VOD is Vodafone on the London Stock Exchange, AAPL is Apple, and XAUUSD is gold priced in US dollars.

Volatility

How much and how quickly a price moves. High volatility means bigger swings in both directions, so more opportunity and more risk.

Liquidity

How easily you can buy or sell something without moving its price. Major currencies and big shares are very liquid; small companies and some crypto are not.

Market cap

A company’s total value on the stock market: the share price multiplied by the number of shares. It’s how “large-cap” and “small-cap” are measured.

Diversification

Spreading your money across different assets, sectors and regions, so one bad investment can’t sink your whole portfolio.

Compounding

Earning returns on your past returns. Reinvested over years, small regular gains can grow into a much larger sum.

Investing

Share (stock)

A small slice of ownership in a company. If the company grows and its profits rise, the share price and any dividends usually follow, and the reverse is true too.

Index

A basket that tracks a section of the market. The FTSE 100 follows the 100 largest companies listed in London; the S&P 500 follows 500 large US companies.

ETF

An exchange-traded fund: a single investment that holds many assets, often tracking an index, and trades on a stock exchange like a share.

Index fund (tracker)

A fund that simply copies an index instead of trying to beat it. Low fees and broad diversification make trackers a common starting point for long-term investors.

Dividend

A share of a company’s profits paid out to shareholders, usually in cash, often twice a year or quarterly.

P/E ratio

Price-to-earnings: the share price divided by the company’s earnings per share. It shows how many years of current profits you’re paying for, and helps compare how expensive shares are.

Earnings season

The weeks each quarter when most listed companies report their results. Share prices can move sharply on whether results beat or miss expectations.

Analyst rating

A buy, hold or sell view on a share published by a bank or research firm, often with a price target. Useful context, but analysts are frequently wrong.

ISA

An Individual Savings Account: a UK tax wrapper. You can put up to £20,000 a year across your ISAs, and the interest, dividends and gains inside are free of UK tax. Allowances can change, so check gov.uk for the current rules.

SIPP

A Self-Invested Personal Pension. You choose the investments, and contributions get tax relief, but you usually can’t access the money until 55 (rising to 57 from 2028).

Pound-cost averaging (DCA)

Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals instead of all at once. You buy more when prices are low and less when they’re high, which smooths out your entry price.

Bond

A loan to a government or company that pays you interest and returns your money at the end of a set term.

Gilt

A bond issued by the UK government. Gilt yields set the tone for UK mortgage rates and borrowing costs.

Yield

The income an investment pays as a percentage of its price. For bonds, yields rise when prices fall, and fall when prices rise.

Trading

Long and short

Going long means profiting if a price rises. Going short means profiting if it falls, usually through derivatives like CFDs or spread bets.

Spread

The gap between the buy (ask) and sell (bid) price. It’s a built-in cost on every trade, and it widens when markets are quiet or volatile.

Pip

The standard unit for measuring moves in currency pairs: usually the fourth decimal place, so 0.0001, or the second for yen pairs.

Leverage

Using borrowed exposure to control a bigger position than your deposit. It magnifies gains and losses equally. In the UK, retail leverage is capped at 30:1 on major currency pairs and 20:1 on gold.

Margin

The deposit a broker holds to keep a leveraged position open. If losses eat into it, the broker can close your positions automatically.

CFD

A contract for difference: a leveraged bet on whether a price will rise or fall, without owning the asset. CFDs are high risk, and most retail traders who use them lose money.

Spread betting

A UK leveraged product similar to a CFD, where you stake an amount per point of movement. Profits are currently free of Capital Gains Tax for most people, but losses can exceed your deposit if you don’t manage risk.

Stop loss

An order that closes your trade automatically at a set price, capping how much you can lose on it.

Take profit

An order that closes your trade automatically once it reaches your target, locking in the gain.

Risk/reward ratio

How much you could gain on a trade compared with how much you could lose. A 1:3 ratio risks £1 to potentially make £3.

Position sizing

Deciding how much to put into a single trade. Many traders risk only 1–2% of their account on any one idea.

Support and resistance

Price levels where buying (support) or selling (resistance) has repeatedly stepped in before. Traders watch them as likely turning points.

Slippage

When your order fills at a worse price than you asked for, usually during fast-moving markets or big news.

Economics and central banks

Inflation

The rate at which prices rise across the economy. High inflation erodes what your money can buy and pushes central banks to raise interest rates.

CPI

The Consumer Price Index, the main measure of inflation. A CPI number above or below forecast is one of the biggest market movers of the month.

Bank Rate

The Bank of England’s main interest rate, set by its Monetary Policy Committee eight times a year. It feeds through to mortgages, savings and the pound.

Central bank

The institution that sets interest rates and manages a country’s money supply: the Bank of England in the UK, the Federal Reserve (the Fed) in the US.

Hawkish and dovish

Hawkish means leaning towards higher interest rates to fight inflation; dovish means leaning towards lower rates to support growth.

Quantitative easing (QE)

When a central bank creates money to buy bonds, pushing down long-term borrowing costs. Quantitative tightening (QT) is the reverse.

GDP

Gross domestic product: the total value of everything an economy produces. It’s the headline measure of whether an economy is growing or shrinking.

Recession

A sustained fall in economic activity. In the UK it’s commonly defined as two consecutive quarters of falling GDP.

Non-farm payrolls (NFP)

The monthly US jobs report, usually released on the first Friday of the month. It regularly moves the dollar, gold and stock markets within seconds.

Safe haven

An asset investors rush to in times of fear, like gold, the US dollar, the Swiss franc or government bonds.

Crypto

Bitcoin

The first and largest cryptocurrency, with a fixed maximum supply of 21 million coins.

Altcoin

Any cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin, from large ones like Ethereum to thousands of small, highly speculative tokens.

Stablecoin

A crypto token designed to hold a steady value, usually pegged to the US dollar. Stable in name, but only as safe as the assets backing it.

Halving

The event roughly every four years when the reward for mining new Bitcoin is cut in half, slowing the rate of new supply.

Crypto wallet

Where you hold the keys to your crypto. Leaving coins on an exchange means trusting that exchange; a self-custody wallet means only you control them.
